## Goal

You synthesize existing research on a focused question, showing patterns, debates, methods, and gaps rather than listing sources one by one.

## Preconditions

- A research question or topic boundary.
- Access to scholarly databases, library search, or Google Scholar.
- A required citation style and any instructor or journal guidance.

## Steps

1. **Define the scope.** Set the topic, population, timeframe, field, and inclusion or exclusion criteria. → *Expect:* you know which studies belong in the review.
2. **Search systematically.** Use database keywords, synonyms, subject headings, and citation chasing from key papers. → *Expect:* you have a documented search path and a pool of relevant sources.
3. **Screen for relevance and quality.** Read abstracts first, then methods and conclusions for likely matches. → *Expect:* weak or off-topic sources are removed.
4. **Take synthesis notes.** Record each source's question, method, sample, findings, limitations, and relation to other studies. → *Expect:* notes are comparable across sources.
5. **Group studies by theme.** Organize by method, theory, chronology, finding, debate, or population rather than by author name. → *Expect:* clusters reveal the structure of the literature.
6. **Identify agreements and tensions.** Note where studies converge, disagree, or use incompatible methods. → *Expect:* the review has analytical points to make.
7. **Write a thesis for the review.** State what the literature collectively shows and where uncertainty remains. → *Expect:* the review has an argument about the field.
8. **Draft thematic sections.** Introduce each theme, synthesize multiple sources, and explain its relevance to the research question. → *Expect:* paragraphs compare sources instead of summarizing one article at a time.
9. **Conclude with gaps and next steps.** Explain what future research or your own project should address. → *Expect:* the ending justifies the next inquiry.
10. **Check citations and coverage.** Verify every claim against the source and format references consistently. → *Expect:* readers can trace all borrowed ideas.

## Decision points

- The assignment asks for an annotated bibliography → summarize and evaluate sources individually instead of writing a thematic synthesis.
- The field is large → narrow by date, population, method, geography, or theory.
- Evidence conflicts → explain methodological differences before declaring one side stronger.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Source-by-source listing:** detect one paragraph per article → reorganize around themes and cite multiple sources per point.
- **F2 Search is too shallow:** detect reliance on the first few web results → use library databases and citation chaining.
- **F3 Scope is unmanageable:** detect dozens of unrelated themes → narrow the research question.
- **F4 Claims outrun evidence:** detect broad statements from one study → qualify the claim or add more sources.

## Verification

The review states a focused scope, synthesizes credible scholarly sources by theme, identifies patterns and gaps, and includes complete in-text citations and a reference list in the required style.

## Variations

- `apa-example`: Smith, J. A., & Lee, R. T. (2024). Sleep and memory in college students. Journal of Learning Science, 18(2), 101-118. https://doi.org/10.0000/example
- `systematic-review`: follow a formal protocol, database log, screening flow, and quality appraisal method.
- `humanities`: books and theoretical works may matter as much as recent journal articles; organize by schools of thought or interpretive debates.

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Do not misrepresent studies, cite abstracts as if you read full papers, or include private participant data from unpublished research without approval.
