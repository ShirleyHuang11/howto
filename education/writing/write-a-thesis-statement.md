## Goal

You produce a focused thesis statement that directly answers the prompt and gives the paper a clear line of reasoning.

## Preconditions

- The exact assignment prompt or research question.
- Enough background reading to understand the main issue.
- Any required stance, source, text, or method from the assignment.

## Steps

1. **Identify the required task word.** Circle verbs such as argue, compare, analyze, evaluate, explain, or interpret. → *Expect:* you know what kind of claim the thesis must make.
2. **Turn the prompt into a question.** Rewrite it as "What should this paper prove or explain?" → *Expect:* the thesis will answer a real question.
3. **Draft a direct answer.** Write one sentence that responds to the question without announcing "this essay will." → *Expect:* you have a claim, not a topic label.
4. **Add the main reason or logic.** Include because, by, through, although, or another structure that shows why the claim holds. → *Expect:* the thesis previews the argument's engine.
5. **Make it specific.** Replace broad nouns and vague judgments with concrete subjects, causes, texts, dates, or criteria. → *Expect:* the thesis could not fit any paper on the topic.
6. **Test for arguability.** Ask whether a reasonable reader could disagree or require evidence. → *Expect:* the thesis needs support rather than stating an obvious fact.
7. **Check scope against the assignment length.** Narrow or broaden the claim so it can be proved in the available pages. → *Expect:* the thesis fits the paper you can actually write.
8. **Revise after outlining.** Adjust the thesis once your evidence and paragraph plan are clearer. → *Expect:* the final thesis matches the essay's actual argument.

## Decision points

- The assignment is explanatory → the thesis may state a central finding rather than a debatable position.
- The assignment is argumentative → the thesis should take a clear stance and imply reasons.
- Your evidence contradicts the first thesis → revise the thesis instead of forcing the evidence to fit.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Thesis is only a topic:** detect "This paper is about climate change" → add a specific claim about cause, effect, interpretation, or policy.
- **F2 Thesis lists facts:** detect a sentence no one would dispute → add significance or interpretation.
- **F3 Thesis is overloaded:** detect multiple unrelated claims joined together → choose the strongest claim and move extras into body paragraphs.
- **F4 Thesis promises evidence you do not have:** detect unsupported claims during outlining → gather better sources or narrow the claim.

## Verification

The thesis answers the prompt, takes a clear position or states a focused explanation, previews the paper's logic, and fits the assignment length.

## Variations

- `literary-analysis`: "In The Great Gatsby, Fitzgerald uses Gatsby's parties to show that wealth creates spectacle but not genuine belonging."
- `research-paper`: include the population, cause, effect, or method when relevant.
- `compare-contrast`: name both subjects and the basis for comparison in the same sentence.

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. A thesis may be improved with feedback, but the final claim should reflect your own argument and should not misrepresent sources.
