## Goal

You find credible scholarly sources that fit a research question and provide enough evidence for an academic paper or project.

## Preconditions

- A topic, research question, or assignment prompt.
- Access to a library database, catalog, Google Scholar, or discipline-specific index.
- The required number and type of sources, if assigned.

## Steps

1. **Break the topic into concepts.** List the main nouns, variables, population, place, and timeframe. → *Expect:* you have searchable building blocks.
2. **Generate keyword variants.** Add synonyms, broader terms, narrower terms, and discipline-specific vocabulary. → *Expect:* searches are not limited to one phrase.
3. **Search scholarly databases.** Use your library discovery tool, Google Scholar, PubMed, ERIC, JSTOR, Scopus, Web of Science, or a field-specific database as appropriate. → *Expect:* results include peer-reviewed articles, books, or conference papers.
4. **Use filters carefully.** Limit by date, peer review, subject, language, or document type only when relevant. → *Expect:* results become more focused without excluding key work unnecessarily.
5. **Read abstracts and subject terms.** Screen for relevance before downloading everything. → *Expect:* you can tell which sources truly match the question.
6. **Follow citation trails.** Check references in a strong source and use "cited by" tools to find newer work. → *Expect:* you discover sources beyond the first search page.
7. **Save full citation details.** Export to a citation manager or notes file with DOI, stable URL, and database information if needed. → *Expect:* sources can be cited later without retracing the search.
8. **Get full text legally.** Use library links, interlibrary loan, open-access versions, or author repositories. → *Expect:* you have readable copies without using unauthorized uploads.
9. **Select a balanced set.** Choose sources that represent major findings, methods, and viewpoints. → *Expect:* the source list supports analysis rather than one-sided cherry-picking.

## Decision points

- You find too few results → broaden terms, remove filters, or ask a librarian for subject headings.
- You find too many results → narrow by population, method, date, theory, or geography.
- You need current evidence → use citation tracking to find recent studies that cite foundational work.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Search terms are too casual:** detect mostly blogs or news → replace everyday words with discipline terms and search databases.
- **F2 Paywall stops progress:** detect inaccessible full text → use library access, interlibrary loan, or legal open versions.
- **F3 Sources do not answer the question:** detect interesting but off-topic articles → revise keywords around the actual research question.
- **F4 Overreliance on one author or viewpoint:** detect a narrow bibliography → search for reviews, critiques, and alternative methods.

## Verification

You have a saved set of scholarly sources that directly address the research question, include complete citation metadata, and represent the key parts of the debate or evidence base.

## Variations

- `health`: use PubMed or CINAHL and pay close attention to study design, sample size, and publication date.
- `education`: use ERIC, PsycINFO, and library databases; distinguish peer-reviewed research from teaching resources.
- `mla-example`: Chen, Rui. "Digital Archives and Public History Pedagogy." Public Historian, vol. 46, no. 1, 2024, pp. 33-57.

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Use legal access routes, avoid predatory journals, and do not submit copyrighted PDFs to public tools unless the license allows it.
