## Goal

You get specific, usable feedback on a draft and turn it into a revision plan.

## Preconditions

- A draft that is complete enough for someone else to understand.
- The assignment prompt, rubric, and due date.
- A trusted reviewer, tutor, instructor, writing center, or peer.

## Steps

1. **Decide what kind of feedback you need.** Choose structure, thesis, evidence, clarity, grammar, citations, or audience fit. → *Expect:* the reviewer knows where to focus.
2. **Prepare the draft and context.** Send the prompt, rubric, due date, and any specific concerns with the draft. → *Expect:* the reviewer can judge the work against the assignment.
3. **Ask targeted questions.** Use questions such as "Where does the argument stop making sense?" or "Which paragraph needs stronger evidence?" → *Expect:* feedback is more specific than "looks good."
4. **Set boundaries for the review.** State whether you want comments only, suggested edits, or a conversation. → *Expect:* the reviewer does not accidentally rewrite the paper for you.
5. **Receive feedback without arguing in the moment.** Take notes, ask clarifying questions, and mark repeated concerns. → *Expect:* you understand the feedback before deciding what to use.
6. **Sort comments by revision priority.** Separate assignment compliance, argument, organization, evidence, style, and proofreading. → *Expect:* high-impact fixes come before small wording changes.
7. **Choose what to accept, adapt, or reject.** Apply feedback that improves the paper and meets the assignment, and note why you are declining other comments. → *Expect:* revision remains your own work.
8. **Make a revision checklist.** Convert comments into concrete tasks with sections or page numbers. → *Expect:* you can revise systematically instead of rereading all comments repeatedly.

## Decision points

- Two reviewers disagree → follow the prompt and instructor expectations; ask the instructor if the conflict affects grading.
- Reviewer rewrites sentences in their own voice → use the idea behind the edit, not the exact wording.
- Feedback arrives too late → prioritize thesis, prompt compliance, evidence, and citation errors.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Vague feedback:** detect comments like "awkward" or "unclear" without location → ask the reviewer to point to one example and explain their confusion.
- **F2 Overediting by reviewer:** detect large rewritten sections → compare to your original meaning and rewrite in your own words.
- **F3 Defensive reaction:** detect yourself dismissing all comments immediately → wait, reread later, and look for patterns.
- **F4 Missed rubric issue:** detect feedback focused only on grammar → separately compare the draft to the rubric before submitting.

## Verification

You have received focused comments, converted them into a prioritized revision checklist, and revised the draft while preserving your own authorship.

## Variations

- `writing-center`: bring the assignment prompt and one or two goals for the session; tutors usually coach rather than edit line by line.
- `peer-review`: exchange drafts early enough that both writers have time to revise.
- `sample-question`: "After reading my introduction, what do you think my main claim is?"

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Share drafts only with trusted people or approved services, and do not ask someone else to produce text you will submit as your own.
