## Goal

You restate a source idea accurately in your own wording and sentence structure while still giving the source credit.

## Preconditions

- The original source passage and page or location information.
- The required citation style.
- A clear reason for using the source in your own argument.

## Steps

1. **Read the passage for meaning.** Identify the claim, evidence, and context before rewriting. → *Expect:* you can explain the point without looking at the source.
2. **Close or hide the source.** Write the idea from memory in your own sentence structure. → *Expect:* your draft is not built by substituting synonyms line by line.
3. **Change both wording and structure.** Combine, split, reorder, or reframe the idea as needed for your paragraph. → *Expect:* the paraphrase sounds like your paper, not the source.
4. **Compare against the original.** Check that the meaning is accurate and the wording is not too close. → *Expect:* no distinctive phrase remains unless quoted.
5. **Add a citation.** Cite the source even though you changed the wording. → *Expect:* readers know where the idea came from.
6. **Introduce the source's role.** Use a signal phrase or explanation showing why the paraphrase matters. → *Expect:* the paraphrase supports your point instead of appearing abruptly.
7. **Quote irreplaceable language.** If a term or phrase is distinctive and necessary, put it in quotation marks. → *Expect:* borrowed wording is visibly marked.
8. **Revise for integration.** Connect the paraphrase to the previous and next sentences. → *Expect:* the paragraph flows as your own analysis.

## Decision points

- The original wording is legally, historically, or literarily important → quote the key phrase instead of paraphrasing it away.
- The passage contains several ideas → paraphrase only the idea you need.
- You cannot explain the passage → reread context or choose a clearer source before paraphrasing.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Synonym swapping:** detect the same sentence order as the source → rewrite with the source closed and restructure the idea.
- **F2 Meaning drift:** detect a paraphrase that exaggerates or changes the claim → correct it to match the source.
- **F3 Citation omitted:** detect paraphrase without a citation → add the proper citation immediately.
- **F4 Overparaphrasing technical terms:** detect changed terms that become inaccurate → keep standard technical terms and cite the source.

## Verification

The paraphrase accurately represents the source, uses your own wording and structure, includes a citation, and fits the logic of your paragraph.

## Variations

- `apa-example`: Original idea: feedback works best when students can revise. Paraphrase: Revision opportunities make feedback more useful because students can immediately apply comments to improve their work (Nguyen & Patel, 2023).
- `mla-example`: Revision opportunities make feedback more useful because students can immediately apply comments to improve their work (Nguyen and Patel 51).
- `technical-writing`: keep accepted technical terms unchanged, but rewrite the explanation and cite the source.

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Proper paraphrasing still requires citation; do not paste confidential sources, unpublished interviews, or private class materials into public rewriting tools.
