## Goal

You write a lab report that states the question, explains the method, presents results honestly, and interprets what the data show.

## Preconditions

- The lab prompt, rubric, and required format.
- Raw data, observations, measurements, and any approved analysis tools.
- The course's policy on collaboration and use of templates or AI tools.

## Steps

1. **Identify the required report structure.** Confirm whether the instructor wants title, abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion, conclusion, references, and appendices. → *Expect:* a section checklist that matches the rubric.
2. **State the research question and hypothesis.** Connect the hypothesis to the scientific principle being tested. → *Expect:* the reader can tell what relationship or effect the lab examined.
3. **Describe the method precisely.** Summarize materials, variables, controls, procedure, and measurement units in past tense. → *Expect:* another student could understand what was done without copying the lab manual word for word.
4. **Clean and label the data.** Organize tables with units, trial labels, significant figures, and any excluded data clearly explained. → *Expect:* the dataset is readable and traceable to the experiment.
5. **Analyze the results.** Calculate required statistics, graphs, uncertainty, error, or percent yield using the course-approved method. → *Expect:* results are reported with correct units and enough detail to verify calculations.
6. **Write the results section without interpretation first.** Present key numerical findings, tables, and figures. → *Expect:* the section reports what happened before explaining why.
7. **Write the discussion.** Explain whether results support the hypothesis, compare to expected theory or literature, identify sources of error, and propose realistic improvements. → *Expect:* interpretation is tied directly to data, not vague claims.
8. **Add citations and references.** Cite the lab manual, textbook, articles, or safety data sheets in the required style. → *Expect:* borrowed methods, facts, and expected values are credited.
9. **Revise for scientific clarity.** Check tense, units, figure references, significant figures, and whether claims exceed the data. → *Expect:* the report reads as precise scientific communication.

## Decision points

- Data contradict the hypothesis → report that honestly and explain plausible reasons; do not alter data.
- A measurement was missed → state the limitation and analyze available data if allowed.
- The course requires passive voice or first person → follow the instructor's style preference.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Unsupported conclusion:** detect a conclusion that does not cite a result → add the relevant data or weaken the claim.
- **F2 Graph is misleading:** detect missing units, truncated axes, or unlabeled series → rebuild the figure with clear labels and caption.
- **F3 Method copied from manual:** detect step-by-step manual language → paraphrase what was actually done and cite the manual.
- **F4 Calculation error:** detect inconsistent units or impossible values → redo calculations from raw data and show formulas where required.

## Verification

The report contains all required sections, every figure and table is labeled and referenced, calculations use correct units, conclusions match the data, and citations follow the assigned style.

## Variations

- `apa-7`: many science courses use APA-like headings and figure captions. EXAMPLE reference: American Psychological Association. (2020). *Publication manual of the American Psychological Association* (7th ed.).
- `chemistry`: include reaction equations, limiting reagent, percent yield, and safety considerations when relevant.
- `biology`: include organism, sample size, controls, and ethical handling details when relevant.

## Safety & privacy

Low writing risk, but scientific integrity matters. Never fabricate data, hide excluded observations, or upload identifiable participant or patient data to outside tools.
