## Goal

You write a clear five-paragraph essay with an introduction, three focused body paragraphs, and a conclusion that answers the prompt.

## Preconditions

- The assignment prompt, length requirement, due date, and citation style if sources are required.
- Any class rubric or teacher instructions.
- Notes, readings, or examples relevant to the topic.

## Steps

1. **Restate the prompt as a task.** Identify what the essay must explain, argue, compare, or analyze. → *Expect:* you can say the assignment in one sentence.
2. **Draft a specific thesis.** Write one sentence that answers the prompt and previews the main reason or position. → *Expect:* the thesis is arguable or explanatory, not just a topic.
3. **Choose three body points.** Pick the three strongest reasons, examples, or parts of the explanation. → *Expect:* each body paragraph has a distinct purpose.
4. **Outline the paragraphs.** Plan introduction, body paragraph 1, body paragraph 2, body paragraph 3, and conclusion. → *Expect:* the essay has a visible structure before drafting.
5. **Write the introduction.** Start with context, narrow to the prompt, and end with the thesis. → *Expect:* a reader knows the topic and your main answer.
6. **Write each body paragraph with evidence.** Use a topic sentence, evidence or example, explanation, and link back to the thesis. → *Expect:* each paragraph supports one main point.
7. **Write the conclusion.** Rephrase the thesis, synthesize the three points, and end with a final implication. → *Expect:* the essay closes without introducing a new argument.
8. **Revise for logic and assignment fit.** Check that every paragraph answers the prompt and appears in the best order. → *Expect:* no paragraph feels off-topic or repetitive.
9. **Edit and cite if needed.** Fix grammar, transitions, formatting, and citations. → *Expect:* the final draft meets the rubric and does not contain uncited borrowed ideas.

## Decision points

- The prompt asks for analysis, not opinion → make each body paragraph explain how or why the evidence matters.
- The teacher allows more than five paragraphs → use the five-paragraph format as a planning scaffold, then expand where the argument needs more space.
- Sources are required → cite every borrowed fact, quotation, paraphrase, or distinctive idea.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Thesis is too broad:** detect a thesis that could support a whole book → narrow it to the assigned text, case, period, or question.
- **F2 Body paragraphs summarize only:** detect paragraphs that retell facts without explaining them → add analysis after each example.
- **F3 Paragraphs overlap:** detect the same reason repeated three times → combine duplicates and add a distinct point.
- **F4 Conclusion repeats mechanically:** detect copied wording from the introduction → synthesize what the argument shows instead.

## Verification

The essay has five coherent paragraphs, a clear thesis at the end of the introduction, three distinct body points with support, and all required formatting and citations.

## Variations

- `timed-exam`: outline for 3-5 minutes, draft quickly, and reserve the final minutes for thesis clarity and paragraph order.
- `literary-analysis`: evidence should include short quotations or details from the text, followed by interpretation.
- `example-thesis`: "School start times should be later because adolescent sleep patterns, attendance data, and classroom performance all show that early schedules work against learning."

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Do not submit someone else's essay or uncited AI-generated text as your own; follow your school's academic integrity policy and keep teacher feedback private.
