## Goal

You organize an argumentative essay so the claim, evidence, reasoning, counterargument, and conclusion work together persuasively.

## Preconditions

- A debatable prompt or research question.
- Credible evidence from assigned texts, data, scholarly sources, or reliable reporting.
- Any required citation style and word count.

## Steps

1. **State the central claim.** Write a working thesis that takes a position and indicates why it matters. → *Expect:* the essay has a defensible main argument.
2. **Define the audience and stakes.** Identify what readers likely know, doubt, or care about. → *Expect:* the argument responds to real reader concerns.
3. **Sort evidence by function.** Label evidence as background, proof for claim, counterargument, limitation, or implication. → *Expect:* each source has a job in the essay.
4. **Choose the strongest order.** [BRANCH: simple case, move from least to strongest reason | complex case, start with the most important issue and handle objections early] → *Expect:* paragraph order builds persuasion intentionally.
5. **Build body paragraphs around reasons.** Start each paragraph with a claim, then evidence, reasoning, and connection to the thesis. → *Expect:* paragraphs argue instead of only reporting information.
6. **Include a fair counterargument.** Present the strongest reasonable objection without mocking it. → *Expect:* readers see that the essay understands the debate.
7. **Respond to the counterargument.** Refute, concede, qualify, or reframe the objection using evidence. → *Expect:* the original thesis becomes more precise and credible.
8. **Write transitions that show logic.** Use transitions for cause, contrast, concession, sequence, and consequence. → *Expect:* readers can follow why one paragraph leads to the next.
9. **Conclude with significance.** Explain what the argument changes about the issue, decision, or interpretation. → *Expect:* the ending does more than repeat the thesis.

## Decision points

- Evidence is mixed → qualify the thesis rather than hiding inconvenient facts.
- The prompt requires a specific structure → follow the assignment format even if another structure seems stronger.
- The audience strongly disagrees → address shared values and counterarguments earlier.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Claim is not debatable:** detect a thesis that only states a fact → revise it into a position about meaning, cause, value, or action.
- **F2 Evidence is dropped in:** detect quotations or data without explanation → add reasoning that connects evidence to the claim.
- **F3 Counterargument is weak:** detect a straw-man objection → replace it with the best objection a serious reader would make.
- **F4 Paragraph order feels random:** detect abrupt topic shifts → reorder by logic and add transition sentences.

## Verification

The essay has a clear thesis, each body paragraph supports a distinct reason, at least one fair counterargument is answered, and every major claim is backed by cited evidence.

## Variations

- `classical`: introduction, background, claim, proof, refutation, conclusion.
- `rogerian`: begin with common ground, represent opposing views carefully, then propose a balanced position.
- `sample-claim`: "Cities should prioritize protected bus lanes over road widening because bus lanes move more people, cost less to maintain, and reduce congestion without inducing additional car traffic."

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Represent opposing views and sources honestly; do not fabricate evidence, hide limitations, or cite sources you have not consulted.
