## Goal

You judge whether a source is credible, relevant, and appropriate for an academic or research task.

## Preconditions

- The source itself, not only a citation or summary.
- The assignment requirements for source type and quality.
- A research question or claim the source might support.

## Steps

1. **Identify the source type and purpose.** Determine whether it is scholarly research, news, opinion, government information, advocacy, commercial content, or reference material. → *Expect:* you understand what the source is trying to do.
2. **Check author expertise.** Look for credentials, institutional affiliation, publication record, or relevant lived or professional experience. → *Expect:* the author has a reason to be trusted on this topic.
3. **Inspect the publisher or platform.** Evaluate the journal, press, organization, website, or database hosting the source. → *Expect:* the publication venue has accountable editorial standards.
4. **Verify evidence and methods.** Look for citations, data, methods, sample, transparency, and limitations. → *Expect:* claims are supported rather than merely asserted.
5. **Check date and currency.** Decide whether the source is current enough for the field and question. → *Expect:* the publication date fits the topic's pace of change.
6. **Look for bias and conflicts.** Identify funding, advocacy goals, sales interests, political framing, or selective evidence. → *Expect:* possible bias is understood and weighed.
7. **Cross-check key claims.** Compare important facts with other credible sources. → *Expect:* major claims are corroborated or disagreements are explainable.
8. **Decide how to use it.** Use the source as evidence, background, a viewpoint to critique, or reject it. → *Expect:* the source has an appropriate role in the project.

## Decision points

- The source is peer-reviewed but outdated → use it for theory or history, then add newer evidence.
- The source is biased but transparent → it may be useful as a primary source or viewpoint, not neutral evidence.
- The source lacks author or publisher accountability → avoid relying on it for important claims.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Confusing professional-looking design with credibility:** detect polished graphics but weak evidence → evaluate authorship, methods, and citations.
- **F2 Using a source outside its expertise:** detect a credible author writing beyond their field → find a specialist source.
- **F3 Ignoring retractions or corrections:** detect controversial or surprising claims → search for corrections, retractions, and later replications.
- **F4 Treating all bias as disqualifying:** detect advocacy sources → use them carefully for perspective while relying on stronger evidence for factual claims.

## Verification

You can explain who created the source, why they are credible or limited, what evidence supports their claims, how current it is, and how it should or should not be used.

## Variations

- `craap-test`: evaluate Currency, Relevance, Authority, Accuracy, and Purpose.
- `sift-method`: Stop, Investigate the source, Find better coverage, and Trace claims to the original context.
- `apa-example`: If accepted, record enough data for a reference such as: Miller, S. Q. (2022). Evaluating online science misinformation. Information Literacy Review, 7(4), 12-29.

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Be cautious with medical, legal, or financial sources; for high-stakes decisions, use current expert guidance rather than a student-level credibility check alone.
