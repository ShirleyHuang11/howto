## Goal

You turn a research question, sources, and notes into a logical paper structure before drafting.

## Preconditions

- A prompt or assignment sheet.
- A working thesis or research question.
- Notes from enough credible sources to support the paper.

## Steps

1. **Restate the assignment in your own words.** Identify the required task, audience, length, source count, and citation style. → *Expect:* a one-sentence description of what the paper must accomplish.
2. **Write a working thesis.** Make a specific, arguable claim that answers the research question. → *Expect:* a thesis that can be supported or challenged, not just a topic.
3. **Group your evidence by idea.** Sort notes into clusters such as background, causes, comparison, counterargument, method, or implications. → *Expect:* 3-6 major sections begin to emerge.
4. **Choose the paper's main structure.** [BRANCH: argumentative, organize around reasons supporting the thesis | analytical, organize around themes or findings | literature review, organize by debate, method, chronology, or theme] → *Expect:* an ordering principle that fits the assignment.
5. **Create section headings with claims.** Turn each section into a sentence that advances the thesis. → *Expect:* headings that say something, not vague labels like "Body paragraph 1."
6. **Place evidence under each claim.** Add source names, data points, quotations, or examples where they will be used. → *Expect:* every section has support and source gaps are visible.
7. **Add counterarguments or limitations.** Identify where you will address opposing evidence, uncertainty, or limits in your sources. → *Expect:* the outline anticipates reasonable objections.
8. **Check flow and proportion.** Estimate paragraph counts or word ranges for each section and move material until the paper builds logically. → *Expect:* no section is overloaded, missing, or out of sequence.
9. **Convert the outline into a drafting plan.** Mark the easiest section to draft first and list any research still needed. → *Expect:* a clear next writing action and a short source-gap list.

## Decision points

- Thesis still reads like a topic → revise it into a claim before outlining further.
- A section has no evidence → research more or remove that claim.
- The assignment requires IMRaD, legal analysis, or another fixed structure → follow that structure first and adapt claims inside it.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Outline mirrors source order:** detect one source per paragraph → regroup by ideas so sources speak to each other.
- **F2 Thesis changes halfway through:** detect sections proving a different claim → revise the thesis or reorder the outline around the actual argument.
- **F3 Missing counterargument:** detect a one-sided outline on a debatable issue → add the strongest opposing view and your response.
- **F4 Too broad for the length:** detect more sections than the paper can support → narrow the thesis by population, time, place, text, or mechanism.

## Verification

The outline has a working thesis, logically ordered claim-based sections, source evidence under each section, a place for counterargument or limitations, and a clear drafting plan.

## Variations

- `apa-7`: headings may be formatted in levels; use them to reflect the hierarchy of claims.
- `mla-9`: outlines are often submitted separately; confirm whether the instructor wants sentence outline or topic outline.
- `sample-thesis`: A defensible thesis could be: "Community college transfer programs improve bachelor's completion most when advising begins before the first semester and continues through credit evaluation."

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Keep private participant data, unpublished class materials, and paid database PDFs out of public outlining tools unless your institution permits it.
