## Goal

You reduce mealtime conflict and expand a child's food acceptance using repeated low-pressure exposure while watching for nutrition, growth, or sensory concerns that need professional help.

## Preconditions

- The child is medically stable and not choking, vomiting frequently, losing weight, or dehydrated.
- Caregivers can offer regular meals and snacks at predictable times.
- You know any allergies, texture restrictions, feeding therapy instructions, or cultural/religious food requirements.

## Steps

1. **Separate normal pickiness from red flags.** Note growth, energy, accepted foods, gagging, vomiting, constipation, swallowing trouble, and extreme texture avoidance. → *Expect:* you know whether this is routine selectivity or needs clinical input.
2. **Set a predictable meal rhythm.** Offer meals and snacks at planned times, with water between and limited grazing. → *Expect:* the child arrives at meals with an appetite.
3. **Serve one safe food with family foods.** Include at least one food the child usually eats alongside small portions of new or less-liked foods. → *Expect:* the child can eat something without the meal becoming a test.
4. **Use tiny exposures.** Offer a pea-size taste, lick, smell, touch, or "learning portion" without pressure to finish. → *Expect:* the child encounters new foods without panic.
5. **Avoid bribes and battles.** Do not force bites, shame, or make dessert the prize for vegetables. → *Expect:* mealtime stays calm enough for learning.
6. **Model eating the food.** Eat the same food yourself and describe neutral sensory facts, not moral judgments. → *Expect:* the child sees normal eating behavior.
7. **Repeat exposures many times.** Reintroduce foods in different preparations over weeks. → *Expect:* rejection once does not remove a food permanently.
8. **Involve the child outside mealtime.** Let them wash produce, stir, choose between two vegetables, or plate a tiny portion. → *Expect:* familiarity increases before tasting.
9. **Track patterns and escalate red flags.** If the diet narrows, growth falters, or meals cause severe distress, contact the pediatrician, dietitian, or feeding therapist. → *Expect:* persistent or serious feeding problems get professional support.

## Decision points

- Child eats fewer than about 10-15 foods, loses accepted foods, or avoids whole textures → seek feeding or medical evaluation.
- Mealtime conflict is intense → reduce pressure first before focusing on new foods.
- Constipation or reflux affects appetite → address the medical issue with the clinician.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Pressure worsens refusal:** detect crying, gagging, or bargaining every meal → stop forced bites and return to exposure without demands.
- **F2 Grazing kills appetite:** detect constant snacks or milk before meals → create meal/snack windows.
- **F3 Nutrition gap emerges:** detect low iron foods, little protein, or no fruits/vegetables → ask a clinician or dietitian about safe supplementation and planning.
- **F4 Parent cooks endless alternatives:** detect multiple replacement meals nightly → include one safe food in the planned meal instead.

## Verification

Meals follow a predictable rhythm, the child has access to at least one accepted food plus low-pressure exposures, growth and hydration are stable, and caregivers know when to seek clinical help.

## Variations

- Toddler: appetite varies day to day; judge intake over several days, not one meal.
- Neurodivergent or sensory-sensitive child: use occupational or feeding therapy strategies and respect sensory distress.
- Allergy family: introduce and rotate foods within the allergy plan from the clinician.

## Safety & privacy

Medium risk because restrictive eating can mask medical, growth, allergy, choking, or sensory problems. Do not force-feed; confirm choking-safe food shapes and seek professional help for weight loss, dehydration, swallowing trouble, or severe restriction.
