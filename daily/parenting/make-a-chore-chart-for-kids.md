## Goal

You create a chore chart that assigns age-appropriate household responsibilities clearly enough for children to follow and adults to maintain.

## Preconditions

- A list of recurring household chores.
- The child's age, abilities, school schedule, and known frustration points.
- Paper, whiteboard, spreadsheet, or app for tracking.

## Steps

1. **List essential recurring chores.** Include only tasks that truly need doing daily or weekly. → *Expect:* the chart is based on real household work, not filler.
2. **Match chores to age and ability.** Choose tasks the child can complete with reasonable teaching, such as putting clothes in a hamper, feeding a pet with supervision, setting the table, or taking out trash. → *Expect:* each task is safe and developmentally realistic.
3. **Define "done" visibly.** Write the exact finish standard, such as "toys in bins and floor clear" instead of "clean room." → *Expect:* the child knows what success looks like.
4. **Keep the chart small.** Start with 2-4 regular responsibilities per child. → *Expect:* the chart feels manageable for the first week.
5. **Choose timing.** Attach chores to routines such as after breakfast, before screens, after dinner, or Sunday reset. → *Expect:* chores have a predictable trigger.
6. **Teach each chore once or twice.** Demonstrate, do it together, then watch the child do it. → *Expect:* the child has practiced before being held responsible.
7. **Add tracking.** Use checkmarks, magnets, stickers, or app taps for completion. → *Expect:* progress is visible without repeated arguments.
8. **Decide rewards or consequences.** Keep them simple, consistent, and connected to privileges if you use them. → *Expect:* everyone knows what happens when chores are done or skipped.
9. **Review weekly.** Adjust tasks that are too hard, too easy, or poorly timed. → *Expect:* the chart improves instead of becoming ignored.

## Decision points

- Child cannot complete the chore after teaching → break it into smaller steps or pick a simpler task.
- Multiple children argue about fairness → rotate disliked tasks and account for age differences openly.
- Rewards become the only motivation → keep some chores framed as family contribution, not paid work.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Chart overload:** detect frequent failure or avoidance → reduce the number of chores and rebuild consistency.
- **F2 Vague standards:** detect adult redoing everything → make the finish standard visible with photos or examples.
- **F3 Parent forgets to check:** detect no marks for several days → attach review to dinner or bedtime.
- **F4 Sibling comparison:** detect resentment over different chores → explain age-based expectations and rotate where possible.

## Verification

Each child has a visible chart with age-appropriate chores, clear completion standards, timing cues, and a weekly review routine.

## Variations

- Preschool: use pictures and one-step jobs.
- Elementary: use checkboxes, rotating weekly jobs, and simple allowance if desired.
- Teen: use shared household expectations, calendars, and larger weekly responsibilities.

## Safety & privacy

Low risk. Avoid chores involving sharp tools, hazardous chemicals, heavy lifting, heat, or unsupervised pet medication unless the child is old enough and trained.
