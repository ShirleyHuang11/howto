## Goal

Recognize altitude sickness early, stop it from worsening, and descend or get medical help when warning signs appear.

## Preconditions

- You are traveling above roughly 2,500 m or sleeping significantly higher than your recent baseline.
- You know your route's sleeping elevations, descent options, weather, and nearest medical help.
- You have water, food, warm layers, and any clinician-prescribed altitude medication if relevant.

## Steps

1. **Check symptoms honestly.** Look for headache, nausea, dizziness, fatigue, poor sleep, appetite loss, shortness of breath, confusion, or coordination problems. → *Expect:* symptoms are named instead of dismissed as normal travel tiredness.
2. **Stop ascent if symptoms start.** Rest at the same elevation, reduce exertion, hydrate, eat lightly, and avoid alcohol or sedatives. → *Expect:* the plan changes from gaining altitude to stabilizing.
3. **Use medication only as directed.** Take acetazolamide, anti-nausea medicine, pain relief, or oxygen only according to clinician or local medical guidance. → *Expect:* treatment supports acclimatization without masking severe decline.
4. **Reassess after rest.** Check whether headache, nausea, walking stability, breathing, and mental clarity improve over several hours. → *Expect:* you know whether symptoms are improving, stable, or worsening.
5. **Descend for worsening or severe signs.** Move lower immediately for confusion, ataxia, breathlessness at rest, blue lips, wet cough, severe weakness, or symptoms that worsen despite rest. → *Expect:* the person reaches a lower altitude or emergency care path.
6. **Delay further ascent after recovery.** Sleep at the same or lower altitude until symptoms fully resolve, then ascend more gradually. → *Expect:* the itinerary resumes only after the body is coping.

## Decision points

- Mild headache only → rest, hydrate, and do not ascend until it improves.
- Symptoms worsen or affect walking or thinking → descend and seek urgent medical help.
- Remote trek with no easy descent → contact guides, rescue, ranger, or emergency services early.
- Prior altitude illness history → discuss preventive medication and ascent limits before the trip.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Symptoms minimized:** travelers blame hangover, food, or poor sleep → treat altitude as the cause until proven otherwise and stop ascent.
- **F2 No descent transport:** roads, weather, or darkness block movement → call local rescue or medical support and use oxygen if available while waiting.
- **F3 Medication masks danger:** pain improves but confusion or breathlessness persists → descend because severe signs override symptom relief.
- **F4 Group pressure:** schedule or summit fever pushes ascent → split the group or abandon the objective because severe altitude illness can be fatal.

## Verification

The traveler with symptoms is no longer ascending, severe warning signs are absent or being treated by descent/emergency care, and further altitude gain waits until symptoms fully resolve.

## Variations

- Ski resorts: rapid car or gondola ascent can trigger symptoms even without trekking.
- High-altitude cities: plan a low-effort first day and avoid alcohol on arrival.
- Guided treks: guides may carry pulse oximeters and oxygen, but symptoms and descent decisions still matter most.

## Safety & privacy

High risk because severe altitude illness can kill. This recipe is not a substitute for medical care; descend and seek urgent help for neurological symptoms, breathlessness at rest, or worsening condition.
