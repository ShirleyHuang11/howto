## Goal

You identify and receive travel-related vaccines and preventive advice early enough for protection and entry requirements.

## Preconditions

- Destination countries, regions, dates, trip length, and activities.
- Current vaccine records and medical history.
- Access to a primary-care clinic, travel clinic, pharmacy, or public-health clinic.

## Steps

1. **Build the itinerary health profile.** List countries, rural or urban areas, altitude, season, lodging, animal exposure, and activities. → *Expect:* vaccine recommendations can be matched to actual exposure risk.
2. **Check official travel health guidance.** Review national public-health travel pages for required and recommended vaccines, malaria advice, and outbreak notices. → *Expect:* you have a destination-specific list of possible vaccines and medicines.
3. **Review your vaccine history.** Find records for routine vaccines such as MMR, tetanus, polio, influenza, COVID-19, hepatitis, and varicella. → *Expect:* missing or outdated routine vaccines are visible.
4. **Book a travel-health appointment early.** Schedule ideally 4 to 8 weeks before departure, or as soon as possible for late travel. → *Expect:* an appointment is set with enough time for multi-dose vaccines where possible.
5. **Discuss risks and contraindications.** Tell the clinician about pregnancy, immune suppression, allergies, medications, prior vaccine reactions, and chronic conditions. → *Expect:* recommendations are adjusted safely to your health status.
6. **Receive required and recommended vaccines.** ⚠️ *Medical confirmation:* confirm the vaccine name, dose, route, and need before administration. → *Expect:* vaccines are given or deferred with a documented reason.
7. **Get proof of vaccination when required.** For yellow fever and some other requirements, obtain the official certificate or record accepted by border authorities. → *Expect:* documentation includes date, vaccine, provider, and official stamp or signature when needed.
8. **Plan follow-up doses and preventive medicine.** Schedule remaining doses and obtain malaria prophylaxis or travel prescriptions if indicated. → *Expect:* reminders and prescriptions cover the full itinerary.

## Decision points

- Departure is soon → still seek advice; some protection or documentation may be better than none.
- Yellow fever risk or transit through a risk country → confirm whether an International Certificate of Vaccination or Prophylaxis is required.
- Immunocompromised or pregnant traveler → do not receive live vaccines without clinician guidance.
- Long-term, rural, or humanitarian travel → ask about rabies, Japanese encephalitis, typhoid, cholera, and extended malaria planning.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 No vaccine records:** detect missing documentation → ask prior clinicians, state registries, schools, employers, or repeat vaccines if clinician recommends.
- **F2 Vaccine unavailable locally:** detect clinic stockout → call travel clinics, pharmacies, or public-health departments early.
- **F3 Multi-dose series cannot finish:** detect departure before final dose → start the series and schedule remaining doses as clinically advised.
- **F4 Certificate error:** detect misspelled name, wrong date, or missing stamp → have the provider correct it before travel.

## Verification

Your vaccine record and any required official certificate show the necessary vaccines, dates, and provider details for the destination and travel dates.

## Variations

- `us`: CDC Travelers' Health lists destination recommendations; yellow fever vaccine must be given by authorized providers.
- `uk`: NHS and NaTHNaC resources are commonly used for destination guidance.
- `last-minute`: a travel clinic can still advise on food, water, insect precautions, malaria medication, and partial immunization.

## Safety & privacy

Medium risk because this involves medical decisions and border requirements. Use official public-health sources and licensed clinicians, disclose relevant health history, and keep vaccine documents private but accessible while traveling.
