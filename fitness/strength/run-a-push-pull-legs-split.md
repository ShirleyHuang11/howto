## Goal

You organize strength training into push, pull, and legs sessions so each muscle group gets enough work and recovery across the week.

## Preconditions

- At least 3 training days per week, and preferably 4-6 only if recovery is solid.
- A training log for exercises, sets, reps, loads, and soreness.
- Basic familiarity with full-body training from `fitness/strength/build-a-beginner-full-body-routine`.
- Warm up before each session with general movement and lighter sets of the first exercises.

## Steps

1. **Choose your weekly frequency.** [BRANCH: 3 days, run push, pull, legs once each | 4-6 days, repeat the sequence and insert rest days as needed] → *Expect:* no muscle group is trained hard again before it has recovered.
2. **Build the push day.** Select chest, shoulder, and triceps work such as bench press or push-ups, overhead press, lateral raises, and triceps extensions. → *Expect:* most work involves pressing away from the body.
3. **Build the pull day.** Select back, rear delts, and biceps work such as rows, pulldowns or pull-ups, face pulls, and curls. → *Expect:* most work involves pulling toward the body.
4. **Build the legs day.** Select squat or leg press, hinge or hamstring curl, single-leg work, calves, and optional core. → *Expect:* quads, hamstrings, glutes, and calves all have planned work.
5. **Set recoverable volume.** Start with 2-4 exercises per session and 2-4 sets per exercise, leaving 1-3 reps in reserve. → *Expect:* sessions challenge you but do not wreck the next workout.
6. **Progress one variable at a time.** Add reps, then small load increases, then sets only if recovery remains good. → *Expect:* the log shows gradual progress without weekly exercise chaos.
7. **Place rest days strategically.** Put rest after legs or after three consecutive training days if fatigue builds. ⚠️ *Safety:* stop escalating frequency if joints ache or performance drops across sessions. → *Expect:* strength is stable or improving and soreness resolves between repeats.

## Decision points

- You only have 3 days → full-body may be more efficient, but PPL can still work as one cycle per week.
- Pulling volume is lower than pushing → add rows, pulldowns, or rear-delt work to protect shoulder balance.
- Legs day affects work or sports too much → reduce sets or split quads and hinges across the week.
- Performance drops for several sessions → add a rest day or deload week.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Six-day jump too soon:** detect soreness and declining lifts after adding days → return to 3-4 days and rebuild.
- **F2 Chest-heavy push day:** detect shoulder discomfort and little upper-back work → keep pull volume at least equal to push volume.
- **F3 Skipped legs:** detect repeated missed legs days → schedule legs before rest or use a simpler full-body plan.
- **F4 No progression rule:** detect changing reps and exercises randomly → use a fixed rep range and add load only after hitting it.
- **F5 Too many isolation lifts:** detect long sessions with weak main lifts → prioritize one or two compounds first.

## Verification

You have a written PPL schedule with exercises, sets, rep ranges, rest days, and a progression rule, and you complete one full push-pull-legs cycle without unresolved joint pain or performance drop.

## Variations

- `easier`: run PPL 3 days per week with 2-3 exercises per session.
- `harder`: run a 5-6 day rotating PPL only if sleep, nutrition, and soreness support it.
- `equipment`: use gym machines, free weights, bands, or bodyweight substitutions for each pattern.
- `at-home`: use push-ups and pike presses, band rows and curls, squats, lunges, hinges, and bridges.

## Safety & privacy

Low risk as a planning structure, but the exercises inside it can become risky if load, fatigue, or frequency exceed recovery. Stop any movement that causes sharp pain, deload when fatigue accumulates, and do not force six-day training if recovery markers worsen. This is general fitness guidance, not medical advice; consult a doctor before starting if you have relevant conditions.
