## Goal

You submit a clear Freedom of Information Act request to the right U.S. federal agency and keep enough records to track, narrow, appeal, or follow up on it.

## Preconditions

- The federal agency likely to hold the records; FOIA does not cover Congress, courts, state agencies, or private companies.
- A focused description of the records, date range, names, offices, case numbers, or subject matter.
- Your contact information and any fee-limit or fee-waiver statement you want to include.

## Steps

1. **Identify the records-holder.** Search the agency's FOIA page or FOIA.gov directory, then confirm the component if the agency has multiple offices. → *Expect:* one agency or component is clearly responsible for the records.
2. **Check whether the records are already public.** Search the agency reading room, reports, datasets, and prior FOIA releases. → *Expect:* either you find the records without filing or confirm the request is still needed.
3. **Draft a specific request.** State that you are making a FOIA request, describe the records, include dates, formats, offices, and exclusions for material you do not need. → *Expect:* the request can be understood without back-and-forth.
4. **Set fee instructions.** Say how much you agree to pay, ask to be contacted before fees exceed that amount, and request a fee waiver only if you can explain the public-interest basis. → *Expect:* the agency has instructions for processing fees.
5. **Submit through the official channel.** Use the agency FOIA portal, FOIA.gov handoff, email address, fax, or mail address listed by the agency. ⚠️ *Irreversible:* once submitted, your name and request text may become part of agency records; remove unnecessary personal details first. → *Expect:* the portal, email, or mailing receipt shows the request was sent.
6. **Save proof and tracking details.** Store the request text, date submitted, confirmation number, agency contact, and any uploaded files. → *Expect:* you can quote the tracking number in follow-up messages.
7. **Respond to clarification or fee letters quickly.** Narrow the scope if the agency says the search is too broad, and confirm or dispute estimated fees before the deadline. → *Expect:* the request stays open and moves to processing.
8. **Review the response and appeal if needed.** Compare released records, exemptions, and search description against your request; follow the agency's appeal instructions by the stated deadline. → *Expect:* you either have the records, a written denial, or a timely administrative appeal.

## Decision points

- Records are about yourself → consider a Privacy Act request or combined FOIA/Privacy Act request.
- Records are held by a state or city agency → use that jurisdiction's public-records law, not federal FOIA.
- Request involves classified, law-enforcement, immigration, or personnel records → expect exemptions, longer review, and possible redactions.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Wrong agency:** no records or referral notice → ask which component may hold them and refile there.
- **F2 Overbroad request:** agency asks for narrowing → reduce the date range, offices, record types, or keywords.
- **F3 Fee estimate too high:** fee letter exceeds your limit → narrow the request or agree in writing to a specific cap.
- **F4 Missed response deadline:** no determination after the statutory period → send a status inquiry, then consider mediation through OGIS or an administrative appeal if appropriate.

## Verification

You have a submitted FOIA request with a confirmation or tracking number, saved request text, and an agency contact or portal page showing the request status.

## Variations

- `us-federal`: FOIA applies to executive branch federal agencies; each agency publishes its own FOIA regulation, portal, and appeal address.
- `state-local`: state public-records laws use different names, deadlines, exemptions, and fee rules.

## Safety & privacy

Medium risk because request text and correspondence may reveal identity, legal, immigration, employment, or investigative details. Do not include Social Security numbers, medical records, passwords, or unrelated personal data unless the agency specifically requires identity verification for your own records.
