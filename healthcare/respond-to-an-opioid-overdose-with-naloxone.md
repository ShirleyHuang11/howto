## Goal

A suspected opioid overdose is treated with naloxone, emergency services are called, and breathing is supported until help arrives.

## Preconditions

- Suspect overdose when a person is unresponsive or very hard to wake, breathing slowly or not at all, has blue or gray lips, pinpoint pupils, or drug paraphernalia nearby.
- Call emergency services immediately for any suspected overdose, even if naloxone is available.
- Use naloxone according to its package directions.
- Avoid contact with loose powders or needles; protect yourself from sharps and unknown substances.
- This is emergency first aid, not a substitute for medical care.

## Steps

1. **Check responsiveness and breathing.** Shout their name if known, tap firmly, and look for normal breathing for no more than 10 seconds. → *Expect:* overdose severity is recognized quickly.
2. **Call emergency services.** Put the phone on speaker and say the person is unresponsive or may have overdosed. → *Expect:* dispatch is connected and guiding you.
3. **Give naloxone.** Use nasal spray or injector exactly as labeled. ⚠️ *Irreversible:* once given, naloxone cannot be retrieved; confirm suspected opioid overdose or unresponsiveness with poor breathing first. → *Expect:* a full dose is delivered.
4. **Start rescue breathing or CPR as directed.** If not breathing normally, follow dispatcher instructions for rescue breaths and chest compressions. → *Expect:* oxygen support begins while naloxone takes effect.
5. **Place in recovery position if breathing.** If they breathe normally but are not fully awake, roll them on their side. → *Expect:* the airway is more protected from vomit.
6. **Watch for response.** Note the time naloxone was given and monitor breathing, color, and alertness. → *Expect:* any improvement or continued danger is tracked.
7. **Give another dose if needed.** If there is no response in 2-3 minutes or breathing worsens again, give another naloxone dose if available. → *Expect:* additional reversal is attempted without stopping breathing support.
8. **Keep them from using more opioids.** If they wake agitated or in withdrawal, explain naloxone may wear off and help is coming. → *Expect:* repeat overdose risk is reduced.
9. **Collect information for responders.** Save empty naloxone packages, timing, substances if known, and medication details without handling dangerous items. → *Expect:* responders get useful facts.
10. **Stay until responders arrive.** Continue monitoring because naloxone can wear off before opioids do. → *Expect:* recurrent breathing failure is noticed.

## Decision points

- Person is not breathing normally → call emergency services, give naloxone, and start CPR or rescue breathing as instructed.
- Person wakes up and wants to leave → encourage them to stay because overdose can return.
- Fentanyl or unknown opioid suspected → still use naloxone and be prepared for repeated doses.
- Needles or powders are present → do not handle them; warn responders.
- No naloxone available → call emergency services and start CPR or rescue breathing as instructed.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Waiting to confirm the drug:** detect delayed action while breathing is poor → recover by calling emergency services and giving naloxone when overdose is suspected.
- **F2 Naloxone wears off:** detect renewed sleepiness or slow breathing → recover by giving another dose if available and continuing support.
- **F3 Rescuer exposed to sharps:** detect loose needles → recover by stepping back, avoiding handling, and warning responders.
- **F4 Person becomes combative:** detect agitation after waking → recover by giving space, speaking calmly, and avoiding restraint unless immediate safety requires distance.

## Verification

Emergency services have been called, naloxone was administered with time noted, breathing is supported or normal, and the person remains monitored until professional responders take over.

## Variations

- Nasal spray: insert nozzle into one nostril and press the plunger once.
- Auto-injector or syringe: follow device instructions and local training.
- Multiple bystanders: one calls, one gives naloxone, one retrieves more naloxone or an AED.

## Safety & privacy

High risk because opioid overdose can stop breathing and naloxone can wear off. Call emergency services every time, avoid sharps and powders, and share only emergency-relevant substance information with responders.
