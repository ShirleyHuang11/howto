## Goal

A possible concussion is recognized, activity is stopped, red flags trigger emergency care, and safe monitoring is arranged.

## Preconditions

- A person had a hit, fall, blast, whiplash, collision, or rapid head movement and now has symptoms.
- Call emergency services for loss of consciousness, seizure, repeated vomiting, worsening headache, confusion, weakness, numbness, slurred speech, unequal pupils, neck pain, blood or fluid from ears or nose, or deteriorating alertness.
- Do not let the person return to sports, driving, risky work, or alcohol use the same day.
- Do not move someone with suspected neck or spine injury unless needed for safety.
- This is first aid guidance, not a medical diagnosis.

## Steps

1. **Stop the activity.** Remove the person from play, work, cycling, driving, or the hazardous setting. → *Expect:* no second impact occurs.
2. **Check immediate danger.** Look for airway issues, severe bleeding, neck pain, or unsafe surroundings. → *Expect:* life threats are recognized first.
3. **Screen for red flags.** Ask about headache, vomiting, vision, weakness, numbness, confusion, memory gap, seizure, and loss of consciousness. → *Expect:* emergency warning signs are identified.
4. **Call emergency services if red flags exist.** Report the injury mechanism and symptoms. → *Expect:* urgent care is activated for dangerous signs.
5. **Keep them resting.** Have them sit or lie comfortably with supervision and minimal stimulation. → *Expect:* symptoms are not worsened by exertion.
6. **Do not give risky medicines.** Avoid alcohol, sedatives, and blood-thinning pain relievers unless a clinician says otherwise. → *Expect:* bleeding or masking symptoms is avoided.
7. **Arrange same-day medical advice.** Contact a clinician, urgent care, athletic trainer, or emergency department for evaluation guidance. → *Expect:* concussion care is not self-cleared.
8. **Monitor for worsening.** Check alertness, headache, vomiting, behavior, balance, and speech over the next hours. → *Expect:* deterioration is caught quickly.
9. **Prevent return to risk.** [BRANCH: wants to continue | agrees to rest] stop same-day return even if they feel better; arrange supervision and transport. → *Expect:* second-injury risk is reduced.
10. **Document the event.** Note time, mechanism, symptoms, loss of consciousness, vomiting, and medicines. → *Expect:* clinicians get accurate history.

## Decision points

- Any red flag appears now or later → call emergency services.
- Child, older adult, anticoagulant use, bleeding disorder, intoxication, or high-energy crash → seek urgent medical advice sooner.
- Symptoms are mild but present → stop activity and get medical evaluation before return.
- Neck pain or neurologic symptoms are present → keep still and call emergency services.
- Person insists they are fine → do not allow return to play, driving, or hazardous work the same day.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Returned to play:** detect re-entry after head impact → recover by removing them and notifying responsible staff or guardian.
- **F2 Red flags minimized:** detect repeated vomiting, confusion, or worsening headache treated as normal → recover by calling emergency services.
- **F3 Left alone:** detect no adult monitoring → recover by arranging observation and emergency instructions.
- **F4 Sedatives or alcohol used:** detect substances that mask symptoms → recover by stopping use and seeking medical advice.

## Verification

The person has stopped risky activity, red flags were checked, emergency services were called for any warning sign, and medical evaluation plus supervised monitoring are arranged before return to normal activity.

## Variations

- Sports setting: follow the team's concussion protocol and require medical clearance before return.
- Workplace injury: report through workplace safety channels after urgent care steps.
- Child injury: notify guardians and school staff, and use pediatric medical advice.

## Safety & privacy

High risk because brain injury can worsen and a second impact can be catastrophic. Treat symptoms seriously, protect medical privacy, and share details with clinicians and responsible caregivers.
