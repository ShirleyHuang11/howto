## Goal

A chemical eye exposure is flushed immediately and escalated to poison control or emergency services without delay.

## Preconditions

- A liquid, powder, vapor, or unknown chemical has contacted an eye.
- Start flushing immediately; do not wait to identify the chemical.
- Call emergency services for vision loss, severe pain, alkali or acid exposure, large exposure, burns to face, breathing symptoms, or inability to flush.
- Remove contact lenses only if they come out easily during flushing.
- This is first aid guidance; professional evaluation is usually needed.

## Steps

1. **Start irrigation immediately.** Hold the affected eye open under clean running water, shower, eyewash station, or poured saline. → *Expect:* water flows across the eye and away from the other eye when possible.
2. **Remove contaminated items.** Take off contaminated gloves, glasses, or clothing near the face while flushing continues. → *Expect:* chemical transfer to the eye is reduced.
3. **Hold eyelids open.** Use clean fingers to keep lids apart and ask the person to roll the eye around. → *Expect:* water reaches under both lids.
4. **Remove contacts if easy.** Let contact lenses wash out or remove them gently only if they do not stick. → *Expect:* irrigation continues without scraping the eye.
5. **Flush for at least 15 minutes.** Continue longer for strong acids, alkalis, unknown chemicals, or dispatcher instructions. → *Expect:* dilution is sustained long enough to reduce injury.
6. **Call for expert help while flushing.** Ask someone to call poison control or emergency services and read the product label or safety data sheet. → *Expect:* guidance is obtained without stopping irrigation.
7. **Avoid neutralizers or drops.** Do not put other chemicals, ointment, or medicated drops in the eye unless instructed by a professional. → *Expect:* no secondary reaction is created.
8. **Cover lightly after flushing.** If transport is needed, place a clean loose shield or cloth over the eye without pressure. → *Expect:* the eye is protected but not compressed.
9. **Bring chemical information.** Take the container, label, or photo if safe to do so. → *Expect:* clinicians can identify the exposure.
10. **Get urgent evaluation.** Go to emergency care or follow poison control instructions after significant exposure. → *Expect:* corneal damage is assessed promptly.

## Decision points

- Chemical is strong acid, alkali, drain cleaner, industrial cleaner, cement, lime, or unknown → flush continuously and call emergency services.
- Only mild irritant with symptoms fully resolved after flushing → still call poison control or a clinician for advice.
- Contact lens is stuck → keep flushing and leave removal to clinicians.
- Both eyes exposed → flush both eyes, prioritizing continuous water flow.
- Person cannot tolerate flushing → call emergency services and keep attempting gentle irrigation.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Delayed flushing:** detect search for labels before water starts → recover by flushing first and identifying chemical during irrigation.
- **F2 Water runs into other eye:** detect flow crossing the nose → recover by tilting the head so contaminated water drains away.
- **F3 Contact lens scraping:** detect painful forced removal → recover by stopping removal and continuing irrigation.
- **F4 Neutralizer added:** detect vinegar, baking soda, or another chemical being used → recover by stopping and flushing with water only.

## Verification

The eye was flushed continuously for at least 15 minutes, poison control or emergency services were contacted for significant exposure or red flags, and urgent evaluation is arranged when indicated.

## Variations

- Workplace eyewash: use the station immediately and have a coworker call emergency services and retrieve the safety data sheet.
- Powder chemical: brush excess powder from skin if safe while irrigation begins, but do not delay flushing the eye.
- Child exposure: use a shower, sink sprayer, or clean cup and keep calling for professional help.

## Safety & privacy

High risk because chemical eye injuries can permanently damage vision. Irrigation takes priority over paperwork, and product details should be shared with poison control and clinicians.
