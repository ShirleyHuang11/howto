## Goal

An animal bite is cleaned, bleeding is controlled, and urgent care is arranged for infection, rabies, tetanus, or severe injury risk.

## Preconditions

- A person was bitten or scratched by a dog, cat, wild animal, bat, or unknown animal.
- Call emergency services for uncontrolled bleeding, deep wounds, face or neck wounds, trouble breathing, shock, or severe attack.
- Seek urgent medical advice for bites that break skin, cat bites, hand bites, wild animal bites, bat exposure, immunocompromised people, or possible rabies.
- Capture animal owner and vaccination information only if safe.
- This is first aid guidance, not a substitute for medical evaluation.

## Steps

1. **Get away from the animal.** Move to a secure area and prevent further bites without chasing or handling the animal. → *Expect:* the attack has stopped.
2. **Control severe bleeding.** Apply firm direct pressure with clean cloth or dressing. → *Expect:* bleeding slows while help is contacted if severe.
3. **Call emergency services for major injury.** Report bite location, bleeding, and animal danger if severe signs are present. → *Expect:* emergency response is activated when needed.
4. **Wash the wound.** Rinse under running water with soap for several minutes if bleeding is controlled. → *Expect:* saliva and debris are reduced.
5. **Remove loose debris.** Let water flush away dirt; do not dig deeply into the wound. → *Expect:* cleaning does not worsen tissue damage.
6. **Cover with a clean dressing.** Pat around the wound dry and cover lightly. → *Expect:* the wound is protected while awaiting care.
7. **Gather animal details.** Record animal type, owner contact, rabies vaccine status, location, and whether the animal can be observed. → *Expect:* rabies risk assessment has useful facts.
8. **Check tetanus status.** Note when the injured person last had a tetanus shot if known. → *Expect:* clinicians can decide on booster needs.
9. **Contact medical care.** [BRANCH: skin broken or high-risk bite | superficial no-break scratch] seek urgent care for broken skin or high-risk exposures; monitor superficial no-break scratches. → *Expect:* infection and rabies prevention are considered.
10. **Watch for infection.** Monitor redness, swelling, pus, fever, red streaks, worsening pain, or reduced movement. → *Expect:* delayed infection is caught early.

## Decision points

- Bite is from a bat, raccoon, skunk, fox, wild mammal, or unknown animal → seek urgent rabies advice.
- Cat bite or hand bite → seek same-day medical care because infection risk is high.
- Wound is deep, gaping, on face, or near a joint → seek urgent medical care.
- Animal is still dangerous → stay away and notify animal control or emergency services.
- Person is immunocompromised, diabetic, pregnant, or a young child → seek medical advice promptly.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Animal pursued:** detect attempts to capture it unsafely → recover by retreating and reporting description/location instead.
- **F2 Wound not washed:** detect only a dry bandage applied → recover by washing with soap and running water if bleeding permits.
- **F3 Rabies risk ignored:** detect wild, bat, or unknown exposure → recover by calling urgent care or public health.
- **F4 Tight bandage:** detect swelling or numbness beyond dressing → recover by loosening and re-covering.

## Verification

Bleeding is controlled, the wound is washed and covered, animal and tetanus details are recorded if available, and medical or emergency help is contacted for any broken-skin or high-risk bite.

## Variations

- Bat found in room with sleeping person, child, or impaired adult: seek rabies advice even without a visible bite.
- Service animal bite: treat medically first, then document owner and vaccination information.
- Workplace bite: follow reporting requirements after first aid and urgent medical assessment.

## Safety & privacy

High risk because animal bites can cause severe injury, infection, tetanus, or rabies. Do not risk another bite to identify the animal, and share necessary exposure details with clinicians and public health.
