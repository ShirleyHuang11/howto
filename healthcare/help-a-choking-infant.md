## Goal

An infant under 1 year with a blocked airway receives age-appropriate choking first aid while emergency services are contacted.

## Preconditions

- The infant is under 1 year old and appears to be choking.
- Call emergency services immediately if the infant cannot cry, cough, or breathe, turns blue or pale, becomes limp, or becomes unresponsive.
- Do not use abdominal thrusts on an infant.
- Do not perform blind finger sweeps.
- This is emergency bystander guidance, not a substitute for pediatric first aid training.

## Steps

1. **Check for severe choking.** Look for weak or silent cough, inability to cry, noisy or absent breathing, blue or pale color, or panic. → *Expect:* severe airway obstruction is recognized.
2. **Call or assign a caller.** Point to a specific person to call emergency services, or call on speaker if alone. → *Expect:* emergency help is being contacted.
3. **Position face down.** Support the infant's head and jaw, place them face down along your forearm, and keep the head lower than the chest. → *Expect:* the airway points downward and the head is supported.
4. **Give five back blows.** Use the heel of your hand to strike firmly between the shoulder blades, checking after each blow. → *Expect:* the object may come out or the infant may cry or cough strongly.
5. **Turn face up safely.** Sandwich the infant between your forearms, support the head, and turn them face up with the head still lower than the chest. → *Expect:* the infant is controlled and supported.
6. **Give five chest thrusts.** Place two fingers on the center of the chest just below the nipple line and press about one-third chest depth. → *Expect:* pressure may force air out and dislodge the object.
7. **Repeat the cycle.** Continue five back blows and five chest thrusts until the object clears, help takes over, or the infant becomes unresponsive. → *Expect:* care continues without long pauses.
8. **Remove only a visible object.** If you can clearly see and grasp the object, remove it gently. → *Expect:* the object is removed without pushing it deeper.
9. **Start CPR if unresponsive.** Put the infant on a firm surface and begin infant CPR if trained or instructed by dispatch. → *Expect:* compressions and rescue guidance begin immediately.
10. **Seek medical evaluation after clearing.** Keep watching breathing and have the infant evaluated after severe choking or chest thrusts. → *Expect:* delayed airway swelling or injury is not missed.

## Decision points

- Infant can cough strongly or cry → do not give blows or thrusts; encourage coughing and monitor.
- Infant cannot cry, cough, or breathe → call emergency services and begin back blows plus chest thrusts.
- Object is visible → remove it only if easily grasped.
- Infant becomes unresponsive → stop choking cycles and start infant CPR with dispatcher guidance.
- Infant is older than 1 year → use child choking guidance, not the infant sequence.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Abdominal thrusts used:** detect hands on the infant's abdomen → recover by stopping and switching to chest thrusts.
- **F2 Head unsupported:** detect head wobbling or neck extension → recover by supporting jaw and head throughout turns.
- **F3 Blind finger sweep:** detect fingers placed in the mouth without seeing an object → recover by stopping because it can worsen obstruction.
- **F4 No emergency call:** detect no one on the phone during severe choking → recover by assigning a caller or calling on speaker.

## Verification

The infant can cry, breathe, or cough effectively, emergency services were called for severe choking, and the infant is monitored until medical help or evaluation occurs.

## Variations

- Alone with no phone in hand: provide about 2 minutes of care, then call emergency services if the obstruction persists.
- Small infant: keep the head lower than the torso using your thigh for support.
- Trained rescuer: follow current pediatric CPR training and dispatcher instructions.

## Safety & privacy

High risk because infant choking can become fatal quickly. Call emergency services for severe choking, avoid abdominal thrusts and blind sweeps, and get pediatric first aid training before an emergency.
