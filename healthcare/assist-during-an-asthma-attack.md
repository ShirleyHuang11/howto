## Goal

A person having asthma symptoms uses their reliever inhaler or prescribed plan while emergency help is called for severe or worsening signs.

## Preconditions

- The person has wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, or shortness of breath consistent with asthma.
- Call emergency services now if they struggle to speak, lips or face look blue or gray, symptoms are severe, the reliever is unavailable, there is no improvement after initial reliever use, or they become drowsy or confused.
- Use only the person's prescribed reliever inhaler or action plan.
- This is first aid guidance, not a diagnosis or substitute for medical care.

## Steps

1. **Move away from triggers.** Help the person leave smoke, fumes, cold air, allergens, or exercise if safe. → *Expect:* exposure stops getting worse.
2. **Sit them upright.** Keep them sitting and leaning slightly forward if comfortable; do not make them lie down. → *Expect:* breathing mechanics are easier.
3. **Reassure and slow activity.** Speak calmly and ask them to take steady breaths without forcing deep breaths. → *Expect:* panic and exertion decrease.
4. **Find the reliever inhaler.** Look for the person's quick-relief inhaler and spacer if available. → *Expect:* the correct medication device is ready.
5. **Help use the inhaler.** Follow the label or action plan, usually one puff at a time through a spacer with breaths between puffs. → *Expect:* medication reaches the lungs.
6. **Start timing response.** Note when medication was taken and watch breathing, speech, color, and alertness. → *Expect:* changes are tracked clearly.
7. **Call if severe or not improving.** [BRANCH: improving | severe or not improving] keep monitoring if improving; call emergency services if severe signs persist or worsen. → *Expect:* escalation is not delayed.
8. **Repeat medication only as directed.** Use the action plan, label, or dispatcher instructions for additional puffs. → *Expect:* medication is not underused or wildly overused.
9. **Prepare for responders.** Gather inhalers, spacer, medication list, allergies, and known triggers. → *Expect:* responders receive relevant information quickly.
10. **Monitor until stable or help arrives.** Stay with the person and watch for exhaustion, confusion, blue color, or worsening breathing. → *Expect:* deterioration is recognized immediately.

## Decision points

- Person cannot speak full sentences, is blue or gray, or is getting exhausted → call emergency services immediately.
- Reliever inhaler is unavailable or empty → call emergency services.
- Symptoms improve quickly and match their usual action plan → continue monitoring and avoid the trigger.
- Symptoms return after initial improvement → follow the action plan and seek urgent medical advice.
- This could be anaphylaxis, chest pain, choking, or panic without known asthma → call emergency services if uncertain or severe.

## Failure modes & recovery

- **F1 Person lies flat:** detect worse breathing or lying position → recover by sitting them upright.
- **F2 Wrong inhaler used:** detect controller/preventer instead of reliever → recover by locating the quick-relief inhaler or calling emergency services.
- **F3 No timing:** detect unknown medication time → recover by noting the current time and estimating previous puffs.
- **F4 Delayed call:** detect severe signs after reliever use → recover by calling emergency services now.

## Verification

The person can speak comfortably, breathing and color are improving, emergency services were called for any severe or non-improving attack, and the person remains monitored until stable or professional care takes over.

## Variations

- Child with school plan: follow the written asthma action plan and notify the guardian.
- Spacer unavailable: help them use the inhaler as prescribed and call emergency services sooner if technique is poor or symptoms are severe.
- Exercise-triggered symptoms: stop exercise and do not resume until fully recovered and medically appropriate.

## Safety & privacy

High risk because severe asthma can be fatal. Use the person's prescribed medicine, call emergency services for red flags, and share only relevant health details with responders.
